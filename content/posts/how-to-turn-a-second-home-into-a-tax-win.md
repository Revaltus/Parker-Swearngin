Picture an HVAC owner in Lee's Summit who just wrapped a strong year and is eyeing a lake house near the Lake of the Ozarks, about two hours south. The listing price gets all the attention. The calendar deserves more. How many nights your family stays, how many nights a guest pays, and how those two numbers compare decide whether the IRS treats the place as a personal residence, a rental business, or a hybrid with strict limits on deductions.

These are the second home tax rules that matter most to Kansas City service business owners: subcontractors, engineers, architects, medical and veterinary practice owners, and anyone whose income arrives unevenly and whose tax bill sometimes arrives as a surprise. Used on purpose, the rules can work in your favor. Used by accident, they create a rental loss you can't touch and a tax bill you didn't plan for.

## The day count that decides everything

The IRS sorts a second home into one of three buckets based on two numbers: days rented at a fair price and days of personal use. The details are in [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527) and [Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415), and the short version fits in a table.

| Days rented | Personal use | How the IRS treats it |
|---|---|---|
| 14 or fewer | Any | Personal residence. Rent is tax-free and not reported. |
| 15 or more | More than the greater of 14 days or 10% of days rented | Residence with rental income. Deductions are capped at rental income, so no loss. |
| 15 or more | 14 days or fewer, or 10% or less of days rented | Rental property. Expenses, depreciation, and a possible loss all come into play. |

The first row is the well-known 14-day rule. Rent your lake house for two weeks during a big event weekend or a holiday and the income is yours, tax-free, with no reporting. Mortgage interest and property taxes stay available as itemized deductions, within the usual limits. Interest is deductible on up to $750,000 of combined acquisition debt for your main home and one second home, and property taxes fall under the state and local tax cap.

The third row is where the planning gets interesting, and where most of the mistakes happen.

## What counts as a personal use day

Personal use is broader than "nights you slept there." A day counts as personal use if any of these apply:

- You or a co-owner use the home.
- A family member uses it, even if they pay rent, unless they pay a fair market rate and use it as their main home.
- Anyone uses it under a swap or home-exchange arrangement.
- It is rented to anyone for less than a fair price, like a friend's discount.
- It is donated to a charity auction and used by the winner.

Days you spend mainly fixing the place up or doing maintenance don't count, as long as the work fills most of the day. That distinction matters. Painting the deck for six hours is a repair day. Painting for an hour and fishing the rest of the afternoon is not. We suggest a simple shared calendar and a short note on each repair day, because the IRS asks for proof of days far more often than owners expect.

## A worked example with 120 rental days

Say a drywall subcontractor buys a $450,000 condo at the lake. The building is worth $360,000 for depreciation purposes, and the land makes up the rest. The family stays 12 days. Renters stay 120 days and pay $24,000 in total. Personal use of 12 days is under the 14-day line, so the IRS treats this as a rental property.

Total days of use are 132, so the rental share is 120 divided by 132, or about 91%. Here is how the numbers look, rounded and for illustration only.

| Item | Full year | Rental share (91%) |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, utilities, cleaning, management | $20,000 | $18,200 |
| Depreciation ($360,000 over 27.5 years) | $13,100 | $11,900 |
| Total deductions | | $30,100 |
| Rental income | | $24,000 |
| Net rental loss | | ($6,100) |

On paper, that is a $6,100 loss against a property that is also gaining equity. Before you celebrate, read the next section.

## Why that loss may not offset your business income

Rental losses fall under the passive activity rules. For most owners, a passive loss can only offset passive income. There is one exception that sounds generous: if you actively participate in the rental, you can deduct up to $25,000 of rental loss against other income. But that allowance starts shrinking when your modified adjusted gross income passes $100,000 and is gone at $150,000.

A subcontractor, an engineer with partner income, or a practice owner who just had a big year is likely above that line. The $6,100 loss in our example would not reduce this year's tax. It would be suspended and carried forward until you have passive income to absorb it or you sell the property.

Two routes can change that, and both need honest documentation:

- **Real estate professional status.** You need 750 or more hours a year in real property trades or businesses, and more than half of all your working time must be there. Hours running your HVAC company, your practice, or your engineering firm do not count toward that test, so this route rarely fits an owner who still works in their own business.
- **Short-term rental treatment.** If the average guest stay is seven days or less, the rental is not treated as a "rental activity" for passive loss purposes. Losses can then be nonpassive if you materially participate, which means real, logged hours handling bookings, guest issues, and upkeep. Hiring a management company for everything usually undercuts this. It is also an area where audits focus on hour logs, so treat the log like a job-cost report.

Neither is a loophole to chase for its own sake. It is a choice to make before you buy, based on how you plan to run the property.

## Second home vs investment property: choose the purpose before you close

The tax result follows the use, so the cleanest approach is to decide what the property is for.

**A true second home** means you use it often, rent it rarely or not at all, and treat any rent as a bonus. You keep the mortgage interest deduction within the limits, and with 14 or fewer rental days the income is tax-free. You skip depreciation, record-keeping, and passive loss headaches.

**An investment property** means you rent it most of the year and keep personal use under the 14-day or 10% limit. You deduct operating costs and depreciation, you report every dollar of rent, and you accept that losses may be suspended.

**The hybrid** is where the trouble starts. Rent it 80 days, use it 35, and you land in the middle bucket. Deductions are capped at rental income and applied in a set order: interest and taxes first, then operating costs, then depreciation. You get the paperwork of a rental and none of the loss benefit.

## What happens when you sell

The sale is where a rental surprises people. The home sale exclusion of up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 for married couples filing jointly) requires that you lived in the home as your main residence for at least two of the five years before the sale. A lake house you visit on weekends doesn't qualify.

If you rented the property, depreciation you claimed lowers your basis. The IRS also reduces basis by depreciation you were entitled to claim, even if you skipped it. That portion is taxed at up to 25% when you sell. Skipping depreciation to keep things simple doesn't avoid the recapture. It only forfeits the deduction.

An owner with a $450,000 purchase price and $60,000 of cumulative depreciation should expect that $60,000 to be taxed at up to 25% on top of any other gain. That is a $15,000 federal bill we would rather you see now than at closing.

## Fitting the property into uneven cash flow

Service business owners rarely have smooth income. Progress billing, slow-paying general contractors, and project-based revenue mean one quarter can look very different from the next. A second home adds fixed costs on top: mortgage, insurance, utilities, and property taxes, due on schedule whether or not a payment came in.

A few habits keep the property from becoming a cash crunch:

- **Add rental income to your estimated payments.** Net rental income is taxable, and it belongs in your quarterly estimates with the rest of your income.
- **Check where the property sits.** Income from a Missouri home is taxed by Missouri even if you live in Kansas, and an out-of-state property can mean a nonresident return. Short-term guests also can trigger state and local lodging taxes.
- **Keep the property out of your business books.** A lake house paid from the operating account muddies your financials and your payroll and 1099 tracking. A separate account and separate entry in your accounting system keeps both sets of numbers readable.

## Common Questions

**Is rental income from a second home taxable if I rent it fewer than 15 days?**

No. Under the 14-day rule, rent from a home you rent for 14 days or fewer during the year is tax-free and not reported. You also can't deduct rental expenses. Mortgage interest and property taxes remain available as itemized deductions, subject to the usual limits.

**How many personal days can I use before the home stops being a rental?**

Your personal use must stay at 14 days or 10% of the days you rent it at a fair price, whichever is greater. If you rent it 120 days, 14 days is the limit because 10% is only 12. Go over, and deductions are capped at rental income.

**Can second home losses offset my business income?**

Usually not. Rental losses are passive, and the $25,000 allowance disappears at $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income. Suspended losses carry forward. Real estate professional status or a qualifying short-term rental with material participation can change the result, but both require careful records.

**Do I get the home sale exclusion on a second home?**

Only if the home was your main residence for at least two of the five years before the sale. A weekend or vacation home normally fails that test. Gain is taxable, and if you rented the property, depreciation recapture is taxed at up to 25%.
