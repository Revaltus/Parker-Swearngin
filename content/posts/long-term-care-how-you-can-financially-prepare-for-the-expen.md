Most owners of HVAC, drywall, concrete, and asphalt businesses have a plan for the truck fleet, the next hire, and the next slow-pay customer. Very few have a plan for the day a parent, a spouse, or they themselves need paid daily care. The bill for that arrives all at once, it is larger than almost anyone guesses, and it lands in the same years when many owners are trying to hand off or wind down the business. Long-term care planning costs are far easier to manage when you treat them as a risk to be funded ahead of time, not a crisis to be solved at the hospital discharge desk.

Here is how we walk Kansas City owners through it: what the care costs, how insurance, veterans benefits, and savings each carry part of the load, and what the tax rules do to the math.

## What long-term care costs and why the number surprises people

Recent national cost surveys put a semi-private nursing home room at roughly $8,000 to $10,000 a month, assisted living near $5,000, and a home health aide working a near full-time schedule around $6,000. Prices in the KC area move with the facility, the level of care, and how much help a person needs with bathing, dressing, eating, and moving around. Treat those figures as a starting point and price specific facilities before you build a plan.

Then multiply by time. A stay of two to four years is common, and some run longer. At $9,500 a month, three years of nursing home care is $342,000. For a couple, one spouse needing care can drain the savings the other spouse needs to live on.

Medicare is not the backstop most people assume. It covers short, skilled recovery stays after a hospitalization, not months or years of custodial help. Medicaid does pay for nursing home care in Missouri and Kansas, but only after you have spent down most of your assets, and your choice of facility narrows. That is why the planning has to happen years before the need.

## Why owners of service businesses face a harder version of this problem

If you run a service business, three things make this planning different from what a salaried employee faces.

**Your income is uneven.** Progress billing, slow-paying customers, and seasonal swings mean cash comes in lumpy. A fixed long-term care premium has to survive a slow quarter, so it needs a line in the budget, not a hope.

**Your body is part of the business.** Owners who still run crews or climb onto roofs may be the first to need care, and they are also the ones whose income stops if they cannot work. Disability coverage and long-term care coverage are separate problems, and both deserve a look.

**Your biggest asset is illiquid.** Many owners expect the sale of the company to fund retirement and care. A buyer, a valuation, and a closing date are not guaranteed to line up with a health event. A plan that only works if the business sells at the right price in the right year is a plan with a hole in it.

A useful exercise is to write down where each dollar of a $350,000 care bill would come from if it arrived next year. If the honest answer is "the business sale" or "we'd figure it out," there is work to do.

## Long-term care insurance vs self-funding

The choice is rarely all one or all the other. Most workable plans blend them. Here is how the main options compare.

| Funding source | Best fit | Main strength | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional long-term care insurance | Owners in their 50s with good health and steady cash flow | Transfers a large, unpredictable cost to an insurer | Premiums can rise, and you may pay for years without using it |
| Hybrid life and long-term care policy | Owners who dislike "use it or lose it" | Pays a death benefit or refund if care is never needed | Higher upfront cost, often a lump sum or short payment period |
| Self-funding from savings and investments | Owners with substantial liquid assets | Full control and flexibility | One long claim can wipe out the portfolio |
| VA Aid and Attendance | Eligible wartime veterans and surviving spouses | Monthly benefit that helps cover care costs | Strict service, medical, and net worth rules |

A rough rule we use in planning conversations: if you have well over a million dollars in liquid assets outside the business, self-funding becomes realistic. If you have far less than that, a single long claim could consume what you have, and insurance starts to earn its premium. The in-between group, which includes plenty of successful owners whose wealth is tied up in equipment, real estate, and the company itself, is where hybrid policies and partial insurance often make sense.

Premium pricing depends heavily on age and health at the time you apply. Waiting until your late 60s can mean higher premiums, fewer options, or a declined application. Older policies have also seen significant rate increases, so we look at the insurer's rate history and ask what happens to your plan if the premium rises 30 percent.

Funding the premium is also a cash flow question. An owner who has already trimmed waste from the business, such as the overlapping software and recurring charges described in our post on [subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep), may find that the money for a premium is already sitting in the budget.

## How the veterans aid and attendance benefit fits

If you or your spouse served in the military, check the [VA Aid and Attendance benefit](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/) before you decide anything else. It is an enhanced monthly amount added to the VA pension for wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities or who live in a nursing home or assisted living facility.

The basics are worth knowing:

- The veteran generally needs at least 90 days of active duty, with at least one day during a wartime period.
- The applicant must have a medical need for help with daily living, or be bedridden or in a facility.
- The benefit is need-based, so the VA looks at income and net worth.
- The VA applies a 36-month look-back on assets transferred for less than fair value, which can create a penalty period.

That last point matters. Families sometimes try to move assets to adult children or into trusts to qualify, and a badly timed transfer can delay benefits for months. Our team includes a CPA who is also a tax attorney (JD, LL.M), and that combination helps when the question is about how assets are titled, not just how they are taxed. Dollar amounts adjust each year, so confirm current rates on the VA page instead of relying on a number from a blog post, including this one.

Aid and Attendance will not cover the full cost of a nursing home. It can, however, fill a meaningful gap in a plan that also includes savings and possibly insurance, which is why we ask about military service early.

## What the tax rules do to the math

Tax treatment can shift the real cost of a plan by thousands of dollars a year. The IRS explains the rules in [Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502), which covers medical expenses including long-term care. A few points matter for our clients.

- Premiums for qualified long-term care insurance can count as medical expenses, but only up to an age-based annual limit, and only to the extent total medical expenses exceed 7.5 percent of adjusted gross income.
- Unreimbursed costs for qualified long-term care services for a chronically ill person can also count toward that threshold.
- Self-employed owners may be able to deduct qualified premiums, within the age-based limits, as part of the self-employed health insurance deduction. That is usually more valuable than the itemized route.

How your business is structured changes the answer. A sole proprietor, an LLC partner, and an S-corp owner holding more than 2 percent of the shares each report these premiums differently, and getting it wrong can mean a missed deduction or a payroll reporting error. Entity Type Analysis is part of our advisory work for exactly this reason: the structure you chose for growth reasons ten years ago may now affect how you can pay for your own protection.

If an adult child pays for a parent's care, a separate question arises about whether the parent qualifies as a dependent for medical expense purposes. That depends on the parent's income and support facts, and we look at it during the personal return, not after.

## A funding timeline that works for a Kansas City business owner

Planning by decade keeps this from feeling overwhelming.

**In your 40s,** focus on the business and on disability coverage. Build liquid savings outside the company, and start a retirement plan if you have not. Our Retirement Plan Analysis helps owners compare options such as a SEP, a solo 401(k), or a plan that covers employees.

**In your 50s,** this is the best window to price long-term care or hybrid coverage, while you are likely to be healthy enough to qualify. Review how your investments are allocated too, since a portfolio that has drifted toward risk is a poor foundation for a known future expense. Our post on [rebalancing a balanced investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) covers the mechanics.

**In your 60s,** pin down the exit plan for the business, review the estate documents, and document military service records if they apply. Confirm who holds power of attorney and healthcare directives before anyone needs them.

**When care is already needed,** move quickly but carefully. Gather income and asset records, check VA eligibility, and talk to a professional before transferring anything.

## Common Questions

**How much should I plan to spend on long-term care?**

A practical planning target is $250,000 to $350,000 per person, based on about three years of care at current national prices. Your own figure should come from pricing real facilities or in-home care near you in the KC area, then adding inflation for the years until you might need it.

**Is long-term care insurance worth it for a small business owner?**

It can be, especially if most of your net worth is tied up in the business and you could not comfortably self-fund a three-year claim. It is less compelling if you hold substantial liquid assets outside the company. Age and health at application drive both price and approval, so the 50s are usually the sweet spot.

**Can I qualify for the veterans aid and attendance benefit if I own a business?**

Possibly. Eligibility turns on wartime service, medical need, and income and net worth, not on whether you own a business. Business assets can count toward net worth, so the structure and value of the company matter. Review your situation before applying and avoid transferring assets without advice because of the 36-month look-back.

**Can I deduct long-term care insurance premiums?**

Often in part. Qualified premiums are subject to age-based limits, and self-employed owners may deduct them above the line instead of itemizing. The rules differ by entity type, so check with your CPA before you buy.
