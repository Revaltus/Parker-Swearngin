Every spring, someone in a Lee's Summit driveway asks their accountant the same question with a straight face: "Can I write off the dog?" The answer for the family pet is no. But a few narrow situations do exist where dog costs reduce your tax bill, and they tend to show up in businesses with something worth protecting, like an equipment yard, a shop full of tools, or a trailer of materials.

This post separates the myth from the real rules. If you own an HVAC company, a drywall crew, a concrete or asphalt outfit, or another service-based business around Kansas City, you may be closer to a legitimate deduction than you think. You may also be closer to an audit flag if the paperwork doesn't match the story.

## Why the family dog is never a deduction

The IRS treats a pet as a personal expense, the same category as your groceries or your cable bill. Food, vet visits, grooming, boarding, and pet insurance for a dog that lives in your home and keeps you company are not deductible. A dog is also not a dependent, no matter how much it feels like one at 6 a.m. on a cold morning.

A few common attempts that fail:

- Deducting dog food because the dog sits in your home office while you work.
- Writing off dog boarding while you travel for a business trip. The trip may be deductible, but the kennel bill is still personal.
- Paying a dog walker so you can work longer hours.
- Claiming a "pet-friendly workplace" deduction for a dog that is really just your pet at the office.

If a dog is there for your comfort, the cost is personal. The deduction only opens up when the dog is doing a job.

## When a guard dog counts as a business expense

Business expenses have to be ordinary and necessary for your trade, which is the standard laid out in [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). A guard dog that protects business property can meet that standard, but only when the dog actually works for the business.

Here is a realistic example. A concrete subcontractor south of Lee's Summit keeps a skid steer, a mixer, forms, and a few trucks in a fenced yard. Total equipment value is about $250,000, and thieves have hit a neighboring yard twice. The owner buys a trained guard dog and keeps it at the yard, with a kennel and a fenced run.

In a typical year that might look like this:

| Expense | Annual cost |
|---|---|
| Food | $600 |
| Veterinary care and vaccinations | $450 |
| Professional training | $1,200 |
| Kennel supplies and signage | $300 |
| **Total** | **$2,550** |

This is only an illustration. At a combined federal income tax and self-employment tax rate near 30 percent, a deduction of that size is worth roughly $765 in tax savings. It is not life-changing money. It is real money, though, and it is legitimate when the facts support it.

The facts that matter:

- **The dog is on the job.** It lives and works at the business location, such as the yard, shop, or lot.
- **There is a genuine security purpose.** Theft history, insurance requirements, or the value of what you are protecting all help.
- **The costs are separate.** Dog expenses run through the business account and are coded in your books, not mixed in with your personal spending.

The purchase price of the dog may be treated as a capital cost rather than an immediate write-off, so we look at depreciation rules before assuming anything. The ongoing costs for food, training, and veterinary care are the easier part.

One caution for owners who run a business out of the home. If the dog guards the property but also sleeps on your couch and plays with your kids, the personal use is obvious to anyone reviewing it. A dog that does double duty requires an honest allocation, and often the right answer is a small percentage or nothing at all. Also call your insurance agent before bringing a guard dog onto a work site. A bite claim can cost far more than any deduction will ever save.

## Service animals and the medical expense deduction

A different set of rules applies when a dog helps a person with a disability. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) allows the costs of buying, training, and maintaining a guide dog or other service animal to be included as medical expenses. The IRS language describes people who are visually impaired, hearing impaired, or have another physical disability. Maintenance includes food, grooming, and veterinary care.

There are two catches.

1. **You must itemize.** If you take the standard deduction, medical expenses do not help you.
2. **The 7.5 percent floor applies.** Only the portion of your total medical expenses above 7.5 percent of your adjusted gross income is deductible. With an AGI of $120,000, the first $9,000 of medical costs produces no deduction.

For a household that already has large medical bills, a service dog can push the total over the line. For others, it may not change the return at all.

Emotional support animals and therapy dogs are a grayer area. A dog trained to perform specific tasks for a documented condition is a different conversation than a dog that simply makes you feel better. If you think your situation qualifies, bring us the diagnosis, the training records, and any physician documentation, and we will tell you how the IRS is likely to view it before you put it on a return. We do not guess on these.

Business owners sometimes ask about a therapy dog that works with patients or clients, such as a facility dog in a medical or veterinary practice. If the dog is part of how the practice delivers its service and is used with patients, the costs can be a business expense. If it is mostly the owner's pet that visits the office, they cannot.

## Working dogs, breeding, and the hobby loss question

Some dogs earn their keep directly. Herding dogs on a working farm, hunting or detection dogs used in a paid business, and breeding operations can all be businesses. The test is whether you operate with a genuine profit motive.

The IRS looks at whether you keep separate books, advertise, carry a business plan, and show a profit in at least three of every five years. A breeder who sells a few litters, spends $14,000 a year, and brings in $3,000 is likely to be treated as a hobbyist. Hobby income is still taxable, but hobby expenses are not deductible.

If the operation is real, the treatment changes a lot. Food, veterinary care, stud fees, registration, facilities, and advertising may all be deductible against that income. ## Records that hold up if the IRS asks

Dog deductions draw attention because they sound like the kind of thing people invent. Because our team handles IRS examinations, we know what examiners ask for. Good records make the conversation short.

Keep these on file:

- **Invoices that name the business.** Vet bills, training invoices, and food purchases should be tied to the business, not mixed into your personal accounts.
- **A short written purpose.** One paragraph explaining why the dog is there, when it was acquired, and what it protects.
- **Proof of the problem.** Police reports, insurance notices, or photos of past break-ins support the security reason.
- **A dedicated account in your books.** A category such as "Site security" keeps the numbers clean and visible at year-end.
- **A simple log if use is mixed.** If you ever need to allocate, a basic record of where the dog spends its time is better than an estimate made in March.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct dog food if my dog stays at my business?**

Only if the dog has a real job there, such as guarding an equipment yard or shop after hours. Food, vet care, and training for a working guard dog can be deductible business expenses. Food for a pet that simply keeps you company at the office is a personal cost.

**Is a service animal tax deduction available for an emotional support dog?**

The IRS language in Publication 502 refers to guide dogs and animals assisting people with visual, hearing, or other physical disabilities. Emotional support animals are a gray area and are reviewed case by case. You would also need to itemize and exceed the 7.5 percent of AGI threshold.

**Can I deduct a dog for a home-based business?**

Rarely. A dog that lives in your home and also guards your property has heavy personal use, and examiners notice that. A deduction usually needs a clear business purpose, a business location, and an honest allocation. Often the allocation is small, and sometimes it is zero.
