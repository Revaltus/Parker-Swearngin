Plenty of HVAC, drywall, concrete, and asphalt owners in Kansas City have a brokerage account they opened in a good year and haven't looked at since. It made sense at the time. Then a few busy seasons went by, the crew grew, a general contractor stretched payment to 75 days, and the account kept quietly doing whatever the market told it to do. A balanced investment portfolio is not something you build once. It drifts, and the drift usually runs in the direction that hurts most.

This post is the tax and cash-flow framework that should sit around your investment decisions. It is general information, not advice about specific securities, and your investment advisor should still choose the funds. Our job as CPAs is to make sure the structure around those choices fits how a service business actually earns and spends money.

## Why your portfolio drifts even when you do nothing

Say you start with $1,000,000 split 60 percent stocks and 40 percent bonds. That is $600,000 and $400,000. Stocks have a strong stretch and gain 40 percent while bonds stay flat. Here is what happens without a single trade:

| | Start | After the rally | Share of portfolio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stocks | $600,000 | $840,000 | about 68% |
| Bonds | $400,000 | $400,000 | about 32% |
| Total | $1,000,000 | $1,240,000 | 100% |

You never chose a 68/32 mix. You chose 60/40. The account now carries more risk than you signed up for, and it will hurt more in the next downturn, which is exactly when an owner needs cash for payroll and sub payments.

The Federal Reserve publishes [research notes on household finance and investment behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm), and a recurring theme in that kind of work is that households often leave allocations alone far longer than they intend. "Set it and forget it" feels disciplined. In practice it is a decision to let the market set your risk for you.

## Your business is already your biggest investment

Most portfolio advice assumes you work for a large employer with a steady paycheck. You don't. Your largest asset is a company whose revenue depends on weather, bid cycles, and how quickly GCs pay.

That matters for asset allocation strategy in two ways.

First, your business income and your portfolio can fall at the same time. A slowdown in commercial build-outs or a wet spring that pushes asphalt and concrete jobs into summer can hit your revenue in the same stretch that markets are down. If your portfolio is heavy in stocks and your company is already exposed to the local cycle, you are doubling up on risk without meaning to.

Second, concentration sneaks in through your own backyard. An owner who also holds a rental property, a few local stocks, and shares in a supplier relationship may have 80 percent of net worth tied to the Kansas City economy. Portfolio diversification means more than owning many funds. It means asking what else in your life moves when your industry moves.

## Build three buckets before you pick a single fund

The most useful thing we do with owners is separate money by job before talking about returns. Three buckets cover it.

**Bucket one: operating reserve.** This is cash in the business that covers payroll, sub payments, materials, and insurance when receivables run late. Suppose your monthly outflow is $85,000. Three months of runway is $255,000. If that cushion lives in an index fund instead of a money market account, a bad month in the market can arrive right when a GC pays late. Keep it boring and liquid.

**Bucket two: tax reserve.** Estimated payments, payroll tax deposits, and year-end liabilities need their own home. Owners who skip this bucket often sell investments at the worst time to cover a tax bill, which can trigger more tax on top of the tax they were trying to pay.

**Bucket three: long-term portfolio.** Only what remains after the first two buckets are full belongs here. This is the money that can ride out a down year, so this is where your risk tolerance investing decisions really apply.

When clean books show your cash position clearly, sizing these buckets takes minutes instead of guesswork. That is one reason bookkeeping, payroll, and tax planning under one roof matters for investing too.

## Match risk tolerance to your cash flow, not a quiz

Risk questionnaires ask how you would feel if your account dropped 20 percent. A better question for a business owner is what happens to the company if your account drops 20 percent in the same quarter your biggest customer pays 60 days late.

Two owners with identical net worth can have very different capacity for risk:

- **A drywall subcontractor** with steady repeat work from three builders, 14 employees, and six months of operating cash can reasonably hold more stocks.
- **An HVAC owner** with heavy summer revenue, thin winter months, and a line of credit used for equipment may need a more conservative mix and a larger reserve.

Neither is wrong. The point is that your allocation should reflect how stable your business income is, how much debt you carry, and when you plan to step back. Timing matters too. An owner planning to sell in eight years has a different horizon than one planning to hand the company to a son or daughter.

## Rebalance with taxes in mind

Portfolio rebalancing is simple in theory: sell what grew too large, buy what shrank. The tax cost is where owners get stuck and where a CPA earns the fee.

Back to our example. To return to 60/40, you would sell about $96,000 of stock and buy bonds. In a taxable account, if half of that $96,000 is gain, you realize $48,000 of long-term capital gain. At a 15 percent federal rate that is roughly $7,200. If your income pushes you into the 20 percent bracket, or into the 3.8 percent net investment income tax range, the bill is higher. State rules also differ across the state line, and plenty of KC owners live in Kansas and work in Missouri or the reverse, so we check both sides.

That does not mean you skip rebalancing. It means you choose the cheapest way to do it. Options include:

- **Redirect new money.** Send fresh contributions to the underweight asset instead of selling the overweight one.
- **Rebalance inside retirement accounts.** Trades in a 401(k), solo 401(k), or IRA do not create current capital gains tax.
- **Harvest losses.** If another holding is down, selling it can offset gains elsewhere. Watch the 30-day wash sale rule so the loss is not disallowed.
- **Give appreciated shares.** If you already support a church, school, or youth league in Lee's Summit, donating appreciated stock instead of cash can move the gain out of your return.

None of these choices is obvious from a brokerage statement alone. They depend on your bracket, your basis, and what else is happening on your return this year.

## Let your entity and retirement plan do some of the work

For business owners, the best rebalancing tool is often the business itself. Retirement plan analysis is part of what we advise on, because the plan you choose changes both your tax bill and your portfolio.

If you operate as an S corporation, your W-2 salary affects how much you can contribute to certain plans, so entity type analysis and retirement planning belong in the same conversation. Once you have W-2 employees on the crew, the plan you pick also affects what you may need to offer them. A SEP IRA that looks cheap for the owner can become expensive when eligible employees must be covered. A solo 401(k) stops being an option once you hire. These are decisions to make before you open the account, not after.

Retirement contributions also give you a built-in way to shift allocation. Directing a year-end contribution into the underweight asset is a rebalance with a tax deduction attached.

For owners with larger estates or a business they hope to pass on, the team includes a CPA who also holds a JD and an LL.M, which helps when portfolio decisions overlap with succession and estate questions. The [AICPA's personal financial planning resources](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/personal-financial-planning) are a good outside reference if you want to see how CPAs approach this kind of planning more broadly.

## A yearly review that takes one meeting

You do not need to watch markets daily. You need a repeatable review. A good one fits in a single sitting and covers:

1. **Cash position.** Is the operating reserve still three or more months of outflow, given payroll growth and slower-paying customers?
2. **Tax projection.** What will you owe this year, and is the tax reserve funded?
3. **Allocation drift.** Is any asset class more than 5 percentage points away from target?
4. **Retirement contributions.** Have you used the plan available to you, and does it still fit your entity and headcount?
5. **Big upcoming events.** Equipment purchases, a second location, a buyout of a partner, or a child starting college.

## Common Questions

**How often should a business owner rebalance a portfolio?**

Most owners do well with one scheduled review per year, plus a check whenever an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from target. Pair the review with your tax projection so you can pick the lowest-cost way to rebalance, such as redirecting contributions or trading inside a retirement account.

**Should I keep extra cash in my business instead of investing?**

Usually yes, up to a point. A reserve of three to six months of payroll, sub payments, and fixed costs protects you when GCs pay late. Money beyond that cushion and your tax reserve is a better fit for the long-term portfolio.

**Does a balanced investment portfolio look different for a business owner?**

Often it does. Your company is already a large, concentrated bet on the local economy, so many owners hold a more conservative mix or a larger cash reserve than a salaried employee of the same net worth. The right split depends on income stability, debt, and your exit timeline.
