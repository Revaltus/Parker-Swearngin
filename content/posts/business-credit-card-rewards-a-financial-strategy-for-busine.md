Most subcontractors we talk with in Lee's Summit and across KC carry a business card because a supply house offered one at the counter, not because anyone compared it to how they spend. That works fine until the card is charging 24% interest on a balance that was supposed to be a short-term bridge. Or until the rewards you've been stacking turn out to be worth less than the fee you paid to earn them.

A business credit card rewards strategy is simple in concept. Pick the card structure that matches your real spending, use the billing cycle to smooth out cash flow, pay the balance in full, and keep the bookkeeping clean enough that the tax side takes care of itself. The details are where owners lose money, so let's walk through them using the kinds of businesses we work with: HVAC, drywall, concrete, and asphalt subcontractors, and other service-based businesses with payroll, fuel, and materials bills that never stop.

## Match the card to your spending, not the sign-up bonus

Sign-up bonuses are loud. Spending patterns are what pay you for the next five years.

Before you compare cards, pull three months of statements from your bank and any cards you already use. Sort the spending into four or five buckets: materials and supplies, fuel, equipment rental or repairs, software and phones, and everything else. For a drywall or HVAC sub, materials usually dominate. For an asphalt or concrete outfit, fuel and equipment costs can rival materials. For a service business with a small field crew, software, advertising, and vehicle costs may lead.

The bucket that is biggest should drive the card you choose. A card that pays 5% on categories you rarely buy is worth less than a plain card that pays something on everything.

## A flat 2% card often beats a category card

Here is a hypothetical HVAC subcontractor spending $22,700 a month on business expenses. Compare a flat 2% cash-back card with a category card that pays 5% on fuel and 1% on everything else.

| Monthly spending | Amount | Flat 2% card | 5% fuel / 1% other card |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fuel | $3,500 | $70 | $175 |
| Materials from suppliers | $18,000 | $360 | $180 |
| Software and phones | $1,200 | $24 | $12 |
| **Total** | **$22,700** | **$454** | **$367** |

The flat card earns about $5,448 a year. The category card earns about $4,404. That's roughly $1,044 a year left on the table, and the category card also has caps and rotating rules to track.

Category cards win when one bucket is a large share of your spend. If fuel were $12,000 of that $22,700, the math flips. The point is to run your own numbers instead of trusting the headline percentage.

Two other checks belong on the list before you apply:

- **Annual fee against earnings.** A $95 fee is easy to justify at $5,000 in annual rewards and hard to justify at $400.
- **Redemption flexibility.** Statement credits and direct deposits are simple to record. Travel points with blackout dates are harder to value and easier to forget.

## Use the statement cycle to bridge slow-paying GCs

The bigger win for many subcontractors is not the 2%. It is timing.

Say your drywall crew buys $14,000 of board and compound on the 3rd. Your card statement closes on the 28th and the payment is due on the 20th of the following month. That is roughly 48 days before cash leaves your account. If your general contractor pays progress billings on net-45 terms, the money from the job arrives about when the card bill does.

That float is a legitimate cash-flow tool. It gives you room to make payroll on time, cover a slow-paying GC, and avoid dipping into a line of credit. The [SBA's guidance on managing your business finances](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/manage-your-finances) treats cash-flow planning as a core habit, and a well-timed card is one practical way to do it.

The key phrase is "paid in full." Float works only if the balance is cleared every cycle.

## Interest erases rewards faster than most owners expect

Take a $10,000 balance carried for one month at a 24% APR. The interest is about $200. Earning 2% rewards on that same $10,000 of spending also yields $200. You've earned nothing and added a debt.

If a job's cash is running late and you expect to carry a balance, rewards should drop out of the decision entirely. Look at the APR, any introductory 0% offers, and whether a business line of credit would cost less. A rewards strategy is for the months when cash is healthy. In a cash crunch, the cheapest money wins.

This is also where a simple 13-week cash forecast pays for itself. When you can see that a $40,000 progress payment lands in week seven, you know whether to float materials on the card or call the bank.

## Are credit card rewards taxable for businesses?

Generally, no. Cash back, points, and miles earned by spending on purchases are usually treated as a rebate that reduces the price you paid, not as income. That treatment is why most owners never see a 1099 for their rewards.

There are exceptions worth knowing:

- Sign-up bonuses that don't require any spending, such as a deposit bonus for opening an account, can be taxable and may be reported to you.
- Referral bonuses and cash prizes are more likely to be income.
- Rewards that you receive personally, or that get redeemed for personal travel, don't turn a personal trip into a business deduction.

The expense itself is deductible when it is ordinary and necessary for the business, regardless of how you earned points on it. Annual card fees and business-related interest are generally deductible too, as long as the card is truly a business card. The [IRS Small Business and Self-Employed Tax Center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) is a good starting point for the recordkeeping rules behind all of this, and we confirm how your specific rewards should be treated before filing.

## Paying subs and suppliers by card changes your 1099 work

This one catches subcontractors off guard. If you pay another sub by credit card, that payment is generally reported by the card processor on a Form 1099-K, not by you on a 1099-NEC. So your 1099 tracking needs to separate card payments from checks and ACH transfers, or you risk filing duplicates or missing forms.

A second cost sits on the other side. Some subs and suppliers add a 2.5% to 3% surcharge for card payments. If you earn 2% and pay 3%, you lost money. Ask before you swipe.

A clean setup handles this at the point of entry:

1. Code card payments to subs in a separate account or with a clear tag.
2. Keep W-9s on file for every sub, regardless of how you pay them.
3. Run a year-end report comparing what you paid by check or ACH against what you paid by card.

## Keep the books clean so rewards do not become a mess

Rewards are only useful if the underlying bookkeeping is accurate. The most common problems we see:

- **Personal and business spending on one card.** It makes expense classification slow and weakens your position if the IRS ever asks questions.
- **Unreconciled card statements.** A charge that is never matched to a bill leads to missing deductions or duplicates.
- **Rewards booked inconsistently.** Cash back recorded as income one month and as an expense reduction the next makes your reports hard to trust.

Pick one method with your bookkeeper and stick to it. Reconcile every card monthly. If a card has a statement credit program, record the credit against the expense category it offsets.

This is the kind of detail that is easy to get right when the same team handles your daily transaction classification, expense tracking, and reporting, because the people who see the card feed are the same people who prepare the return.

## Set rules for employee cards before the first surprise charge

If you issue cards to foremen, project managers, or office staff, add controls on day one:

- Set individual spending limits that match each person's job, such as $2,500 a month for a crew lead who buys fuel and small tools.
- Turn on real-time transaction alerts.
- Require a photo of the receipt within a set number of days, ideally through your accounting software's receipt capture.
- Review a list of all card holders every quarter, and close cards for anyone who has left.

Employee cards add to your rewards total and give you one clean data feed instead of reimbursement requests. They also introduce risk, which is why the review step matters.

## Review the strategy once a year

Your spending changes. A new truck, a bigger crew, a move into commercial work, or a second location can shift which card fits best. Once a year, usually early in the year, we recommend three checks:

1. Compare last year's rewards earned against fees and interest paid.
2. Re-sort spending into categories and see whether the biggest bucket has changed.
3. Confirm that your tax planning, including estimated payments, reflects any change in deductible spending.

## Common Questions

**Are credit card rewards taxable for businesses?**

Generally no. Rewards earned by spending are usually treated as a rebate that lowers the cost of what you bought. Sign-up bonuses that require no spending, referral payments, and cash prizes can be taxable. We review the card agreement and your records before deciding how to report anything unusual.

**How do I choose a business credit card for a small service business?**

Start with three months of statements and total your spending by category. If no single category exceeds about 40% of the total, a flat 2% card is usually the safer pick. Then compare annual fees, APR, employee card controls, and how easily the card feeds into your accounting software.

**Should I put payments to subcontractors on my card?**

Only if the sub does not add a surcharge above your reward rate. Card payments to subs are generally reported on a 1099-K by the processor, so you must track them apart from check and ACH payments to avoid duplicate or missing 1099-NEC filings.
