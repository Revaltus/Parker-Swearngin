Most business owners start the housing question with the monthly payment and stop there. A $3,450 mortgage next to a $2,300 rent check looks like an easy no, until someone mentions equity, and then it looks like an easy yes. Both reactions skip the same thing: what the purchase does to your balance sheet and your cash flow. To judge buy vs rent home financially, you have to treat it the way a CPA treats any large commitment, as a line on your personal financial statement.

This post walks through one realistic Lee's Summit example, then shows where service business owners (HVAC companies, concrete and asphalt subs, and other owner-operators with lumpy income) tend to get surprised.

## Start with your personal financial statement, not the listing

Before you open a rent vs buy calculator, write down four numbers:

- **Liquid cash** outside the business, meaning money you could reach in 30 days without a tax hit or a business disruption.
- **Monthly personal cash flow**, after taxes, in your worst three months of the last year, not your average.
- **Debts**, including the truck loan, the equipment note, and any personal guarantee on a business line of credit.
- **Net worth today**, with the business valued conservatively.

The Federal Reserve's [Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf20.pdf) shows why homeownership gets so much attention. In the 2019 survey, median net worth for homeowners was roughly $255,000, against about $6,300 for renters. That gap is real. It is also partly a story about who buys homes: people with stable income, savings, and time. The data does not say that buying makes a household wealthier in every case. It says the households that buy tend to be in a position to.

Your statement tells you whether you are in that position now, or whether a purchase would turn a healthy balance sheet into one that is mostly drywall and a mortgage.

## What a $400,000 home in Lee's Summit costs in year one

Here is an illustration. These are assumptions for the example, not a quote, so swap in your own numbers.

- Purchase price: $400,000
- Down payment: 10% ($40,000)
- Closing costs: about 3% ($12,000)
- Mortgage: $360,000 at 6.75% for 30 years, a principal and interest payment of about $2,335
- Property tax: about 1.3% of value (confirm the current rate for the specific address with the county)
- Insurance: $2,400 a year
- Maintenance: 1% of value a year
- Mortgage insurance: about $1,800 a year at 10% down
- Appreciation: 3% a year, which is an assumption and never a promise
- Comparable rent: $2,300 a month
- Return on cash you would otherwise keep invested: 5%

| First-year item | Buying | Renting |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage interest | $24,200 | |
| Rent | | $27,600 |
| Property tax | $5,200 | |
| Homeowner's or renter's insurance | $2,400 | $200 |
| Maintenance | $4,000 | |
| Mortgage insurance | $1,800 | |
| Lost return on $52,000 cash outlay | $2,600 | |
| **Total cost** | **$40,200** | **$27,800** |
| Less appreciation (3%) | ($12,000) | |
| **Net cost** | **$28,200** | **$27,800** |

The payment on paper is $3,450 a month for the house. The net cost over the first year, once you count appreciation and the return on the cash you tied up, is about the same as renting. Principal paydown is not in the table because it is not a cost. It is a transfer from your checking account to your equity, about $3,800 in year one. But it is also cash you cannot spend.

That is the useful finding. Buying is not wildly better or worse here. It is close, which means the deciding factors are liquidity, how long you stay, and what else that $52,000 could do.

## Why the down payment is the real question for business owners

A salaried employee can usually treat a down payment as savings. A business owner has competing uses for the same dollars: a working capital cushion, a second truck, payroll for a slow-paying month, or a retirement contribution.

There is also a tax wrinkle. If the $52,000 comes from the business, it has to leave as salary or a distribution first. At a combined marginal rate around 30%, you need roughly $74,000 of pre-tax profit to net $52,000. That changes the math on how fast you can realistically buy.

Then compare the alternatives dollar for dollar:

- Paying down a business line of credit at 9% is a guaranteed 9% return, better than the 5% we assumed above.
- Funding a retirement plan can produce an immediate tax deduction. Retirement plan analysis is part of the advisory work our team does with owners, because the right plan depends on your entity type and your payroll.
- Holding six months of personal and business fixed costs in cash protects you from the cash crunch that sends many owners looking for a new accountant in the first place.

If buying a home drains all three, the house is expensive no matter what the listing price says. If you want to see how the investment side fits, our post on [why a set-it-and-forget-it portfolio drifts](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) covers how to think about the money you are not putting into the house.

## How self-employment income changes the mortgage conversation

Lenders look at self-employed borrowers differently. Most want two years of tax returns and qualify you on net income after deductions, not on the revenue you deposit.

That creates a trap. An owner who has done a good job lowering taxable income, through depreciation, equipment purchases, and legitimate expenses, may look like they earn far less than they do. A Schedule C showing $62,000 of net profit on a business that grossed $480,000 will qualify for a much smaller loan than the owner expects.

S-corporation owners have a related issue. Qualifying income often leans on the W-2 salary plus the average of distributions, so a low salary set for payroll tax reasons can limit what a lender will approve. Neither choice is wrong. They just pull in opposite directions, and it is far better to know that 12 to 24 months before applying than the week you find a house.

This is where entity type analysis and tax planning meet your housing plans. Sometimes the right move is to hold off on one more big equipment write-off. Sometimes it is to keep the books current so a lender sees clean, month-by-month profit and loss statements. Sometimes the answer is to rent for another two years while the income history catches up.

## The tax benefit is smaller than most people assume

Mortgage interest is deductible only if you itemize, and the rules and limits are laid out in [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505). In our example, first-year interest of $24,200 plus $5,200 of property tax comes to $29,400. The standard deduction for married couples filing jointly has been close to $30,000 in recent years, so the house adds little or nothing on top of it unless you also have substantial state income tax, charitable giving, or other itemized deductions. State and local tax deductions are capped as well. For a single filer, the standard deduction is about half as large, and itemizing is more likely to pay off.

So a tax deduction should be a small adjustment to your analysis, not the reason you buy. Check the current standard deduction and limits for your filing year before you rely on any figure here.

A business use of the home is a separate topic. If you work from a dedicated space, some costs may be deductible, and the rules differ for renters and owners. It is worth modeling, but it rarely changes the answer by itself.

## The horizon test: how long will you stay?

Buying has big costs at both ends. You paid about $12,000 to close. When you sell, agent commissions and closing costs commonly run 6% to 8% of the price.

Take the same house, held three years at 3% appreciation. It is worth about $437,000, a gain of $37,000. Selling costs at 7% are about $30,600. Add the $12,000 you paid to buy, and the appreciation is entirely consumed. You would have built roughly $12,000 of principal equity, and that is your real gain, against a period when a renter kept more cash free.

Stretch the horizon to seven or ten years and the picture changes. Fixed principal and interest stays flat while rent typically rises, appreciation compounds, and the one-time costs are spread out. For most households, the break-even point lands somewhere between four and seven years. Your own number depends on your rate, your price, and local rent growth.

If your business might relocate, you might sell it, or you plan to expand into a second location within five years, that is a reason to be cautious about a purchase.

## A rent vs buy calculator is only as good as its inputs

Online calculators are a fine starting point. Most of them go wrong in the same places: they assume you itemize, ignore maintenance, leave out the return on your down payment, and use appreciation numbers pulled from a good market. They also have no idea that your income arrives in uneven chunks.

Run your own version with three scenarios: appreciation at 0%, 3%, and 5%. If buying only wins at 5%, you are making a market bet, not a financial plan. If it wins at 0% over your holding period, the decision is much more robust.

Then run the monthly stress test. Take the full cost of ownership ($3,450 in our example) and ask whether you could carry it through your three worst months without touching the business or running up a card. That is the question that matters when a large customer pays 75 days late.

## Common Questions

**Is renting better than buying a home?**

Not automatically. In our $400,000 Lee's Summit example, the net first-year cost of buying ($28,200) and renting ($27,800) was nearly identical. Buying tends to pull ahead the longer you stay, usually after four to seven years, and only if you keep enough cash in reserve.

**How much should I put down if I own a business?**

Put down what leaves you with six months of combined personal and business fixed costs in cash. Ten percent plus mortgage insurance often beats 20% down if the extra $40,000 would otherwise sit in the house while your business line of credit charges 9%.

**Does the mortgage interest deduction make buying worth it?**

Usually not on its own. You only benefit if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction, which is close to $30,000 for married couples filing jointly. Many households with a $360,000 mortgage still take the standard deduction, so treat any tax savings as a bonus.
