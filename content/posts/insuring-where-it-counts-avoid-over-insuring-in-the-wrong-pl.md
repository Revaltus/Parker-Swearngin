Most owners we talk to about insurance can name every policy they carry and still can't say what a bad year would cost them. The agent sold a product, the renewal came in, and the premium got paid. Nobody ever put the exposure next to the balance sheet and asked whether the coverage fits.

That is the question behind "how much insurance coverage do you need." The answer is not a list of policies. It is a calculation built on your net worth, your monthly cash flow, and the size of the hit you could absorb without changing your life or closing your doors. An insurance agent is the right person to place the coverage. A CPA is the right person to size it, because we already know what your books, your tax return, and your cash position say.

## Start with what you cannot afford to lose

Before comparing quotes, answer three questions in dollars.

1. **What is your net worth, and how much of it is reachable?** Business equity, equipment, the building, savings, and your home equity all count. Retirement accounts and homestead rules may shield part of that in Missouri or Kansas, but the protection has limits, and your attorney should confirm what applies to you.
2. **What does one month of fixed cost run?** Payroll, truck payments, rent, insurance, loan service, and owner pay. This is your burn rate, and it tells you how long the business survives if revenue stops.
3. **How much cash could you pull together within 30 days without borrowing?** That is your self-insurance capacity.

The gap between a plausible loss and your self-insurance capacity is the amount you should transfer to an insurer. Anything smaller than your cash cushion, you can often carry yourself. Anything that would wipe out net worth or break payroll, you should not.

That simple frame changes the shopping. You stop buying coverage because it appears on a checklist and start buying it because a specific loss would hurt.

## Where service businesses in Kansas City are exposed

The contractors, professional firms, and practices we serve around Lee's Summit share one trait: their risk comes from people, vehicles, and equipment moving around on other people's property. A drywall crew in a client's house, an HVAC van on I-470, an engineer's stamped drawings, a vet's surgical suite. Each one points to different coverage questions.

The table below is how we frame them with clients.

| Exposure | The sizing question | Common over-spend | Common gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| General liability | What would one injury or property damage claim on a job site cost, including legal defense? | Duplicate coverage across overlapping policies | Limits too low for the contracts you sign |
| Workers' compensation | What is your payroll by job class, and are subs classified correctly? | Misclassified employees, which inflates premium | Uninsured subcontractors who become your liability |
| Commercial auto | How many vehicles, how many drivers, and what is the cost of one serious at-fault accident? | Low deductibles on older trucks | Personal auto policy used for business trips |
| Professional liability | What is the largest fee-at-risk project you carry? | Limits far above any single project's value | No coverage at all for design or advice errors |
| Disability and key person | If the owner is out for six months, what happens to payroll and debt service? | Cash-value policies bought mainly for the tax story | No income protection on the one person who runs the company |

Notice that the two columns on the right point in opposite directions. Over-insuring and under-insuring usually happen in the same business at the same time. The money goes to small, frequent, affordable risks, and the large ones stay thin.

## A worked example from an HVAC subcontractor

Here is an illustrative scenario with round numbers, not a real client.

An HVAC subcontractor in Lee's Summit runs $2.4 million in annual revenue with 14 employees and six service trucks. Fixed costs come to about $140,000 a month. General contractors pay on progress billing, so receivables often sit 45 to 60 days. The owner keeps roughly $90,000 in cash.

The owner's policy on the trucks carries a $1,000 deductible. Moving to a $5,000 deductible would cut the annual premium by about $1,800, according to a quote the agent runs. The extra exposure is $4,000 per claim. If the fleet files a collision claim less often than once every 2.2 years, the higher deductible wins on average. And $5,000 is a small fraction of $90,000 in cash, so one claim does not threaten payroll.

Now look at the other side. The same owner carries a general liability limit that matches the minimum a GC's contract demands, and nothing more. A single serious injury claim, with defense costs, could run far past that limit. The business assets, the equipment, and the owner's home equity would be on the table.

The move here is clear. Take on more of the small, predictable risk through higher deductibles, and redirect that premium savings toward higher liability limits or an umbrella policy. Total spend stays about the same. Protection shifts to where the damage would actually be.

## Sizing the big policies by net worth and cash flow

Net worth is a starting point for liability limits, not a ceiling. Judgments can reach future income in some situations, and legal defense costs can run to six figures before a verdict. A rough method that works for many owner-operators:

- Add up business equity, equipment, real estate, savings, and non-exempt investments.
- Add a cushion for defense costs and for income you will earn in the next several years.
- Compare that to the limits across your general liability, auto, and umbrella policies combined.

If the total of your limits sits well below that number, ask your agent what a higher umbrella layer costs. Umbrella coverage tends to be inexpensive per dollar of protection compared with raising primary limits, which is why it is often the most efficient place to add.

For life and disability insurance, the math runs on cash flow. Life coverage should replace the income your family needs for a defined stretch, pay off debt you personally guaranteed, and fund any buy-sell agreement with a partner. Disability coverage should replace enough of your pay that the mortgage and the business's personal guarantees stay current. For a sole owner, the person is the most valuable asset on the balance sheet, and it is the one most often left uninsured.

## The places owners over-insure

It helps to name these, because they feel responsible when you buy them.

- **Low deductibles on everything.** A $500 deductible feels safe. It also means you are paying a premium to protect against losses your cash can easily cover.
- **Equipment coverage above replacement value.** Insuring a ten-year-old skid steer or compressor at what you paid for it, rather than what it would cost to replace, wastes premium.
- **Overlapping policies.** A business owner's policy, a separate equipment floater, and a rider on a commercial package can cover the same loss twice. Ask your agent to map overlaps.
- **Extended warranties and small-ticket protection plans.** If a failure would not strain cash flow, self-insure it.
- **Cash-value life insurance bought mainly for tax reasons.** It can have a role in some estates, but ask for the numbers on cost, cash value, and the alternative of investing the difference. We run that comparison with clients before they sign.

## What the tax return says about your premiums

Premiums for most business insurance, including liability, property, commercial auto, and workers' compensation, are generally deductible as ordinary business expenses. [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) lays out what qualifies and where the exceptions fall. One that catches owners: premiums on life insurance where the business or the owner is a direct or indirect beneficiary are generally not deductible.

Vehicles raise a related question. If you are deciding between leasing and buying trucks, the insurance cost and the deductible you choose belong in that analysis. Our breakdown of [leasing versus buying a vehicle for a Kansas City service business](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense) covers the tax and cash flow side.

Entity structure also interacts with coverage. An LLC or S-corp can separate business liabilities from personal ones, but it does not cover your own negligence, personal guarantees on loans, or a pierced veil from sloppy bookkeeping. Part of our business advisory work is Entity Type Analysis, and we routinely look at insurance alongside it so the legal structure and the coverage fit together.

## How to run your own needs analysis this month

You do not need a spreadsheet the size of a textbook. Gather these items and sit down for an hour:

1. Your most recent balance sheet and the last three months of cash flow.
2. Every policy declaration page, with limits, deductibles, and renewal dates.
3. Contracts from your top three customers or GCs, with their insurance requirements highlighted.
4. A list of personal guarantees on loans, leases, and credit lines.

Then mark each policy with the loss it protects against and whether you could pay that loss from cash. Policies protecting losses you can absorb are candidates for a higher deductible or a cut. Losses that would exceed cash are candidates for higher limits.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do you need for a service business?**

Enough that your largest realistic loss would not erase your net worth or stop payroll. Start by comparing your fixed monthly costs and cash on hand to the cost of one serious injury, accident, or equipment loss. Cover the gap with insurance. Carry the small stuff yourself through higher deductibles.

**Is it better to have a higher deductible?**

Often, yes, if you have cash to cover it. A deductible increase of $4,000 that saves $1,800 a year pays off if you file a claim less than once every 2.2 years. The test is whether one claim at that deductible would strain your cash. If not, raise it.

**Are business insurance premiums tax deductible?**

Most are. General liability, property, commercial auto, and workers' compensation premiums are generally deductible business expenses, as described in IRS Publication 535. Life insurance premiums are generally not deductible when you or the business benefit from the policy. Confirm your own situation with your CPA before filing.
