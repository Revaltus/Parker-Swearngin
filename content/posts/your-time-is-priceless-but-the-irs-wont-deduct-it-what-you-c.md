You spent forty hours this fall hauling folding tables, running a concession stand, or sitting through board meetings for a Lee's Summit youth league. At an $85 billing rate, that is $3,400 of work you gave away. The IRS will not let you deduct a dime of it. But the gas you burned, the supplies you bought, and the uniform you paid for are a different story, and plenty of business owners leave that money on the table because they assume none of it counts.

Here is how the rules actually work, with examples drawn from the service business owners we see around KC.

## Why the IRS ignores your hours

The tax code lets you deduct what you give, and your time is not something you give in a way the IRS can measure. [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526) says plainly that you cannot deduct the value of your services, no matter how skilled the work or how much you would normally charge for it.

This catches service business owners off guard. If you run an HVAC company and spend a Saturday servicing a church furnace for free, your instinct says the job was worth $600. The return says it was worth zero. The same goes for an engineer who donates a site review to a local charity or a veterinarian who runs a free vaccine clinic.

You also cannot deduct the lost income from time you didn't bill. Skipping a paying job to volunteer is a generous choice, but it is not a tax event.

## The costs that do count

The IRS draws a clear line: unreimbursed out-of-pocket costs are deductible when you incur them while serving a qualified charitable organization. Three conditions apply. The expense must be directly connected with the volunteer work, you must have paid it only because of that work, and it cannot be a personal, living, or family expense.

The organization also has to be qualified. A 501(c)(3) public charity, a school, or a religious congregation generally works. A neighbor's fundraiser or a crowdfunding page for a friend does not. The IRS keeps a [Charities and Nonprofits resource hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) with a search tool, and checking it takes two minutes.

Here is how common volunteer costs sort out.

| Expense | Deductible? | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| Miles driven in your own vehicle | Yes | 14 cents per mile, or actual gas and oil |
| Parking and tolls | Yes | Add to either mileage method |
| Supplies you buy and hand to the charity | Yes | Keep the receipt |
| Uniform the charity requires, not wearable day to day | Yes | Cost and cleaning |
| Meals and lodging on a volunteer trip away from home | Sometimes | Only if there is no significant personal vacation element |
| Your hourly rate or lost billable time | No | Value of services is never deductible |
| Child care while you volunteer | No | Personal expense |
| Gala dinner ticket | Partly | Only the amount above the value of what you received |

That last row trips up a lot of people at year-end giving season. If a $150 ticket buys a dinner worth $60, the charitable portion is $90, and the organization should tell you that number in writing.

## Mileage: the 14-cent rule and when actual costs win

Charitable mileage is not the same as business mileage. Congress set the charitable rate by statute at 14 cents per mile, and it does not move with gas prices the way the business rate does. For a sedan, that is usually your best option.

But if you drive a heavy pickup, the math changes. You are allowed to deduct actual gas and oil instead of the standard rate. You cannot include depreciation, insurance, registration, or general repairs.

Take a drywall or concrete subcontractor who drives a three-quarter-ton truck that gets about 12 miles per gallon. He helps at a Lee's Summit food pantry 30 times a year, a 22-mile round trip each time.

- Total miles: 660
- Standard rate: 660 × $0.14 = $92.40
- Actual gas at $3.30 per gallon: 55 gallons × $3.30 = $181.50

The actual-cost method roughly doubles the deduction, but only if he can prove it. That means a log showing the date, the destination, the purpose, and the miles for every trip, plus fuel receipts or card statements. Pick one method per charity per year and stay consistent. We covered how mileage records and vehicle choices interact in our post on [leasing vs buying a car for a Kansas City service business](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense), and the logging habits carry over.

## Where service business owners get tangled

Volunteering is different when you own a company. Three situations come up again and again.

**Donated services and materials.** Say your asphalt company patches the parking lot at a community center for free. The value of your crew's labor is not deductible. Materials are murkier. If you paid for supplies personally and gave them to the charity, that is an out-of-pocket cost. If the materials came out of business stock, the rules for donated business property apply, and you cannot also claim the same cost as a business expense. Counting it twice is a classic audit trigger.

**Employee time.** If you let a two-person crew volunteer on the clock, the wages are not a charitable deduction. How those wages are treated on the business side is a question for your tax preparer, not something to guess at on a spreadsheet.

**Sponsorships.** Putting your company name on a Lee's Summit youth baseball jersey is usually advertising, not charity, because you get visibility in return. Advertising is a business deduction and gets tracked in a different place than a gift. Writing it off the wrong way will not always cost you money, but it can distort your books and your return.

The common thread is that the line between personal, business, and charitable spending needs to be drawn before the money goes out. It is the same discipline behind whether [a dog can ever be a tax deduction](/resources/unless-its-working-for-you-your-dog-isnt-a-tax-deduction): the purpose of the expense decides where it belongs.

## A few more places owners miss money

Some volunteer costs hide in plain sight.

A real estate broker who chairs a charity auction might pay for printing, postage, and decorations out of pocket. A medical professional who staffs a free clinic might buy scrubs the clinic requires, or a veterinarian might buy a branded jacket for a rescue's adoption days. An attorney or architect on a charity board might drive to a dozen meetings and pay for parking downtown every time.

Each one is small. Together, across a year of serious volunteering, they can reach several hundred dollars.

None of these are loopholes. They are ordinary, allowed deductions that disappear simply because nobody wrote them down at the time.

## Records the IRS expects

The documentation rules scale with the dollar amount, and Publication 526 spells them out.

- **Under $250 in a single contribution:** Keep your own records, meaning receipts, bank or card statements, and your mileage log.
- **$250 or more in out-of-pocket expenses for a single charity:** You also need a written acknowledgment from the organization. It must describe the services you provided and say whether the charity gave you any goods or services in return. You must have this in hand by the time you file, or by the filing due date including extensions, whichever is earlier.
- **Cash gifts:** A bank record or written receipt from the charity is required for any amount.

A workable system is simple. Open a note on your phone titled with the charity's name and add each trip as it happens. Pay for supplies with a card used only for personal expenses so the statement tells the story. When you hit roughly $250 for the year, email the volunteer coordinator and ask for the acknowledgment letter while it is fresh in their mind.

If your business bookkeeping is already in order, add a line in your accounting system for personal charitable costs you track for your return. That keeps volunteer spending out of business accounts, which matters. It is also the sort of setup we handle for clients as part of accounting system setup, so the information is waiting when tax planning begins.

## Itemizing decides whether any of this matters

Here is the caveat that gets skipped in most volunteer deduction articles. Charitable deductions only help if you itemize. Most households take the standard deduction, and $300 in volunteer mileage and supplies will not change that on its own.

The question to ask is whether volunteer costs, cash gifts to charity, mortgage interest, and state and local taxes together exceed your standard deduction. For a business owner with a Lee's Summit mortgage and regular charitable giving, the answer is sometimes yes and often close. In close years, we model both ways and sometimes time gifts so they land in one tax year instead of splitting across two.

If you own an S corporation or an LLC taxed as a partnership, your share of any charitable gifts the business makes passes through to your personal return. A C corporation follows different limits. Individual limits generally cap cash gifts to public charities at a percentage of adjusted gross income, and Publication 526 explains how excess amounts carry forward.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the hours I volunteer at my normal billing rate?**

No. The IRS does not allow a deduction for the value of your time or services, even if you are a CPA, attorney, or electrician donating work you would normally bill at $100 an hour or more. Only unreimbursed out-of-pocket costs tied directly to the volunteer work qualify.

**What is the charitable mileage rate?**

It is 14 cents per mile, set by law, and it is separate from the business mileage rate. You can instead deduct actual gas and oil costs if that produces a larger number and you have fuel records. Parking and tolls can be added to either method.

**Do I need a letter from the charity for my volunteer expenses?**

If your unreimbursed expenses for one organization total $250 or more, yes. The letter must describe your services and state whether you received anything in return. Below $250, your own receipts and a dated mileage log are generally enough.
