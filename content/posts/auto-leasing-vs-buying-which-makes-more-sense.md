Most service business owners in KC don't choose a truck by running numbers. They choose it because the old one quit halfway through a job, and a dealership is offering a monthly payment that sounds manageable. Then the first tax season arrives, the mileage log is blank, and nobody can say whether leasing or buying was the better call.

This post is the framework our CPA-led team walks through with HVAC, drywall, concrete, and asphalt subcontractors, plus engineers, vets, and other owner-operators around Lee's Summit. It starts with how you drive and how cash moves through your business. The sales pitch comes last.

## Start with miles, not the monthly payment

Leases are priced around a mileage allowance, usually 10,000 to 15,000 miles a year. Service businesses blow past that without trying. A tech running service calls from Lee's Summit to Raymore, Blue Springs, and downtown KC can put 25,000 to 30,000 miles on a van in a year.

Here is what that costs on a lease. Say you drive 28,000 miles a year against a 12,000-mile allowance, over a 36-month term. That is 48,000 excess miles. At a typical overage charge of 25 cents a mile, you owe about $12,000 at turn-in. The low monthly payment stops looking low.

The second question is what you do to the vehicle. Ladder racks, shelving, a company wrap, and a bed full of drywall all leave marks. Lessors charge for wear beyond "normal," and a work truck has a different idea of normal than a sales car. If the vehicle earns its living on a job site, ownership usually fits better. If it mostly carries you to estimates and client meetings, leasing gets more interesting.

## What buying does for your taxes

When you buy a vehicle for the business, you recover its cost through depreciation. You can also use the standard mileage rate instead, which the IRS resets each year (70 cents per mile for 2025). You pick one method per vehicle, and your first-year choice limits your later options.

The Section 179 vehicle deduction is where buying can pull ahead. It lets you deduct a large part of the purchase price in the first year instead of spreading it over several. The rules matter, though:

- **Business use must be above 50%.** You deduct only the business percentage, and you need a mileage log to back it up.
- **Weight changes everything.** Passenger cars face annual depreciation caps that hold first-year write-offs to roughly $12,000 to $20,000 in recent years, depending on bonus depreciation. SUVs over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight face a separate Section 179 cap in the low $30,000s. Heavy pickups with a bed of at least six feet and cargo vans without rear seating generally escape those caps.
- **Your income limits the deduction.** Section 179 cannot create a loss beyond your business's taxable income for the year.
- **Recapture is real.** If business use later drops to 50% or below, part of the deduction can come back as income.

Bonus depreciation rules have also shifted in recent years, so the first-year number depends on the date you place the vehicle in service. [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lays out the Section 179 and depreciation mechanics. Confirm current limits with us before you sign anything.

## What leasing does for your taxes

Lease payments are deductible in proportion to business use. If you use the vehicle 90% for business and pay $850 a month, you deduct $9,180 for the year. The deduction is smooth and predictable, with no big first-year swing.

Two details trip people up. First, on higher-value vehicles the IRS requires a small "inclusion amount" added back to income, which trims the deduction. Second, if you use the standard mileage rate on a leased vehicle, you must stick with it for the whole lease term.

The keyword "car leasing tax deductions" brings up plenty of claims that leasing is the better write-off. Usually it is the smaller one. Leasing wins on cash flow and simplicity, not on first-year deductions.

## A $58,000 truck, bought and leased side by side

Here is an illustration, not a quote. An HVAC company owner is looking at a $58,000 heavy-duty pickup that qualifies for full Section 179 treatment. Business use is 90%. Assume a combined federal and Missouri marginal rate of 30%, a 60-month loan at 7%, and a comparable 36-month lease.

| | Buy with a loan | Lease |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment | about $1,150 | about $850 |
| Year-one payments | about $13,800 | $10,200 |
| Year-one business deduction | up to $52,200 under Section 179 | $9,180 |
| Estimated year-one tax savings at 30% | about $15,660 | about $2,754 |
| Mileage limit | None | Typically 10,000 to 15,000 per year |
| End of term | You own it | Return it, or buy at the residual |

On paper, buying produces a much bigger first-year tax break. But a $15,660 tax savings still means you spent $52,200 to save it. The deduction only helps if you have taxable income to absorb it and the truck is something you would buy anyway.

Over the first three years, the lease costs about $30,600 and leaves you with nothing. The loan costs about $41,400 in payments over the same period, but you hold a truck that may still carry meaningful resale value. Heavy-duty trucks tend to hold value well, though that is a market bet and not a guarantee.

The lender's rate matters too. The Federal Reserve's [G.19 consumer credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) publishes what banks and finance companies are charging on new car loans, which makes a handy reality check before you accept the dealership's financing.

## When leasing wins for a service business

Leasing tends to be the better answer in a few specific situations:

- **The vehicle is mostly for you, not the crew.** Owner and sales vehicles that stay under 15,000 miles a year fit a lease allowance comfortably.
- **Cash is tight and the deduction can't be used.** If you had a thin year, a big Section 179 write-off may produce little benefit, and the lower payment frees up cash.
- **You want a newer vehicle every three years.** Warranty coverage through the full term means fewer surprise repair bills.
- **Income is uneven.** For owners who wait on slow-paying general contractors and progress billing, a fixed lower payment is easier to plan around than a loan payment that never shrinks.

## When buying wins

Buying usually comes out ahead when the vehicle works hard. High mileage, upfitting, and a plan to run the truck for seven or eight years all favor ownership. It also fits a strong profit year when you want a legitimate deduction to offset income, as long as the purchase makes sense without the tax break.

A quick test: if you would buy this truck even if the deduction disappeared, the deduction is a bonus. If the deduction is the only reason, stop and rerun the numbers.

## Where the truck fits in your bigger financial plan

A vehicle decision touches more than one line on your return. Our business advisory work around it usually covers four things.

**Entity Type Analysis.** Whether the vehicle sits in your S corporation, your LLC, or your personal name changes how deductions, reimbursements, and personal use are handled. Getting that wrong can turn a deduction into a payroll tax issue.

**Accounting System Setup.** A vehicle should be recorded as an asset, with the loan and mileage tracked in your books from day one. Clean records are what make the deduction hold up if the IRS ever asks.

**Retirement Plan Analysis.** A $1,150 truck payment competes with a 401(k) or SEP contribution for the same dollars. We model both so you do not starve one to feed the other.

**Credit Card Rewards.** If you are financing fuel, tires, or maintenance, the card you use for those expenses matters. Some rewards structures are worth building into the plan, others cost more in interest than they return.

Missouri also taxes vehicles as personal property each year, and lease contracts usually pass that cost to you. Check the paperwork for who pays what before you sign.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the full cost of a vehicle with Section 179?**

Only if it qualifies. Business use must exceed 50%, and you deduct only the business percentage. Heavy pickups and cargo vans generally avoid the annual caps that restrict passenger cars and SUVs. Your taxable business income also limits the deduction, so a slow year can shrink the benefit.

**Is leasing a car cheaper than buying for a small business?**

Leasing usually has the lower monthly payment and the smaller first-year deduction. Buying costs more per month but builds ownership value and can produce a larger write-off. The better choice depends on annual mileage, how much wear the vehicle takes, and whether you can use a big deduction this year.

**What happens if I drive more miles than my lease allows?**

You pay for each excess mile at turn-in, commonly 15 to 30 cents. Driving 16,000 miles a year over the allowance for three years at 25 cents a mile adds about $12,000. If your work routinely exceeds 15,000 miles a year, buying or negotiating a higher allowance upfront is usually the safer route.
