Picture the owner of a Lee's Summit HVAC company in the middle of July. Every truck is running, two general contractors are 60 days behind on payment, and then a spouse's emergency surgery lands a $14,000 out-of-pocket bill. The business account is tight, the personal savings account is thinner than it should be, and the tax return is the last thing anyone is thinking about. It should be the first. How you plan for unexpected medical expenses decides whether that bill is a bad month or a three-year financial hangover.

Most advice stops at "build an emergency fund," and that advice is fine as far as it goes. It just doesn't go far enough for people who run service businesses, where income swings with job schedules and payment cycles. The stronger defense is a set of tax-advantaged tools set up before anything goes wrong.

## Why a savings cushion only goes so far

The Federal Reserve's [Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/report-economic-well-being-us-households.htm) has found, year after year, that more than a third of adults would struggle to cover a $400 surprise expense with cash. Medical costs show up regularly among the unexpected expenses households report. Business owners are not exempt. They are often more exposed, because their cash is tied up in payroll, materials, and receivables.

A standard emergency fund has three weaknesses for a drywall, concrete, or asphalt owner:

- **It is built with after-tax dollars.** Every dollar you save was already taxed at your highest marginal rate.
- **It competes with the business.** When a slow-paying GC stretches your cash, the personal cushion quietly becomes working capital.
- **It is sized by guesswork.** Most people pick a round number, like three months of expenses, that has no connection to what their health plan can actually charge them in a bad year.

A $15,000 cushion helps. A $15,000 cushion that also cut your tax bill by $4,000 helps a lot more. That is the gap tax-advantaged planning fills.

## Size the cushion to your plan, not to a rule of thumb

Start with one number: your health plan's annual out-of-pocket maximum. That is the most you can owe in covered, in-network care in a year. For 2025, federal rules cap that figure at $9,200 for self-only coverage and $18,400 for family coverage on ACA-compliant plans. High-deductible plans that qualify for an HSA have a slightly lower ceiling, $8,300 self-only and $16,600 family.

Your real exposure is the lesser of your out-of-pocket maximum and what you can fund, plus anything out of network or uncovered. Most owners we talk to have never looked up their plan's maximum. Pull it up this week. That one number turns a vague worry into a funding target.

## The HSA is the most underused tool in a business owner's kit

If you are covered by a qualifying high-deductible health plan, a Health Savings Account gives you three tax breaks at once. Contributions are deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. No other account does all three.

| 2025 HSA figures | Self-only coverage | Family coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum contribution | $4,300 | $8,550 |
| Catch-up (age 55 and older) | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| Minimum plan deductible | $1,650 | $3,300 |
| Maximum out-of-pocket | $8,300 | $16,600 |

Limits change every year, so we confirm the current numbers before setting a contribution plan.

Here is what it looks like in practice. A married HVAC owner with family coverage contributes the full $8,550. At a 24 percent federal bracket and Missouri's top rate of 4.7 percent, that deduction is worth roughly $2,400 in income tax savings. The money sits in the account, rolls over every year, and is there on the day the surgery bill arrives. If the family never has a big medical year, nothing is lost. The balance keeps growing, and the same account can pay for qualified expenses in retirement.

Some details matter:

- **Eligibility is strict.** You generally cannot contribute if you are also covered by a non-HSA plan, including a spouse's general-purpose FSA.
- **Owner type changes the mechanics.** Sole proprietors take the deduction on their personal return. S-corp owners holding more than 2 percent of the company have special reporting rules, which is one reason entity structure belongs in this conversation.
- **Invested HSA balances are long-term money.** Once you have a cash buffer for the deductible, the rest can be invested. If you are weighing how that fits with your other accounts, our post on [why set-it-and-forget-it costs a balanced portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) covers the rebalancing side.

## Running it through payroll when you have a crew

If you employ a field crew or office staff, the same logic applies to them, and payroll is where it gets practical. Employer HSA contributions made through a cafeteria plan are exempt from income tax withholding and from FICA. Employee contributions made by payroll deduction get the same treatment. On a $4,000 contribution, the 7.65 percent employer share of payroll tax alone is about $306 saved per person.

A health FSA works differently. It is employer-sponsored, funded by salary reduction, and capped at $3,300 for 2025, with up to $660 allowed to carry over if your plan permits it. It is a good fit for predictable costs like dental work, prescriptions, and vision. It is a poor place to park your emergency money, because unused balances can be forfeited.

For employers under 50 employees, a QSEHRA (qualified small employer health reimbursement arrangement) lets the business reimburse employees for premiums and medical costs tax-free, without a group plan. It can make sense for a 12-person asphalt or concrete company that wants to offer something without taking on a group policy.

## The medical expense deduction is a backstop, not a plan

The itemized medical expense deduction gets a lot of attention, and it helps fewer people than they expect. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) is clear: you can deduct only the portion of unreimbursed medical expenses that exceeds 7.5 percent of your adjusted gross income, and only if you itemize.

Run the numbers for a married couple with $110,000 of AGI:

- The 7.5 percent threshold is $8,250.
- They pay $19,000 out of pocket for a surgery and follow-up care.
- The deductible amount is $10,750.

That looks strong until you compare it to the standard deduction, which is $31,500 for married couples filing jointly in 2025. Unless mortgage interest, state taxes, and charitable gifts already push itemized deductions near that level, the medical deduction adds nothing. Many owners in a rough medical year find they have spent $19,000 and received no tax benefit at all.

This is why the order of operations matters. An HSA deduction is taken above the line, so it works whether you itemize or not. The itemized deduction is the backstop for what the HSA could not cover.

A few ways to make the backstop work harder:

- **Bunch elective care into one year.** If you know a procedure is coming, schedule related care in the same calendar year so you clear the 7.5 percent floor once instead of missing it twice.
- **Track the less obvious costs.** Mileage to medical appointments (21 cents per mile for 2025), lodging for treatment away from home within IRS limits, and insurance premiums you pay with after-tax dollars may all count.
- **Do not double dip.** Expenses paid from an HSA distribution cannot also be deducted.
- **Keep clean records.** Sloppy paperwork is the easiest way to lose a deduction in an IRS examination. Our accounting team categorizes medical spending as it happens so the year-end total is ready.

## A year-round plan in four steps

Here is the order we walk owners through, usually in a single planning meeting:

1. **Find your numbers.** Pull the out-of-pocket maximum and deductible for every covered person.
2. **Confirm HSA eligibility.** Check the plan, check the spouse's coverage, and set a monthly contribution.
3. **Build the cash layer.** Target the gap between your HSA balance and your out-of-pocket maximum, held somewhere separate from business operating cash.
4. **Review each quarter.** Compare estimated tax payments, contributions, and actual medical spending so there are no surprises in April.

## Common Questions

**How much should a business owner save for unexpected medical expenses?**

Aim for your plan's annual out-of-pocket maximum, funded first through an HSA if you qualify. For a family on a 2025 HSA-eligible plan, that ceiling is $16,600. Many owners reach it over two to three years by contributing the HSA maximum of $8,550 annually.

**Is the medical expense tax deduction worth it for a small business owner?**

Only if you itemize and your unreimbursed costs exceed 7.5 percent of AGI. With a $31,500 standard deduction for joint filers in 2025, most owners get no benefit unless they have large mortgage interest or other itemized deductions. An HSA works regardless of whether you itemize.

**Can I open an HSA if I'm self-employed?**

Yes, if you are covered by a qualifying high-deductible health plan and have no disqualifying coverage. Sole proprietors deduct contributions on their personal return. S-corp owners with more than 2 percent ownership have special reporting rules, so check the setup with your CPA before contributing.
